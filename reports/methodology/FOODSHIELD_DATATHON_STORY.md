# FOODSHIELD — DataThon Story

## One-Line Project Statement
FOODSHIELD evaluates how well existing, historical, and new trade relationships can replace a country's food supply after a sudden loss of its dominant supplier.

## 30-Second Pitch
When a country loses its largest food supplier, concentration alone doesn't tell us if they can recover. FOODSHIELD models this specific Rank-1 shock across 10,953 scenarios for six key commodities. The key result is surprising: under historical expansion proxies, 99.83% of the lost supply can be replaced, mostly by current trading partners. However, we identified 224 systems repeatedly dependent on new origins and 9 that face structural constraints. This matters because it shifts focus from fearing concentration to monitoring the resilience of trade network pathways.

## 2-Minute Pitch
A country can appear highly vulnerable because a large share of its food imports comes from a single foreign supplier. But concentration is only half the story. The real question is: "What happens when that dominant supplier disappears?"
Using FAOSTAT data, we modeled a Rank-1 supplier shock across six commodities. We removed the largest supplier for each importer in every year and tested whether the remaining loss could be replaced using historically observed export expansion capacities.
The modeled shock is severe—averaging a 67.17% loss of supply. But the key result is that 99.83% of this lost supply can be replaced on average. Most importantly, 87.04% of these scenarios are resolved through current suppliers alone.
However, aggregate replacement doesn't mean universal security. We found 224 country-commodity systems that repeatedly rely on entirely new origins, and 9 systems where even new origins cannot fill the gap.
When we stress-test the model by cutting expansion capacity to 25%, the overall replacement rate barely drops, but the pathways become much harder, forcing many systems to seek new origins.
The policy meaning is clear: concentration tells us where a shock starts, but trade-network structure tells us how recovery can occur. We must focus on those persistent structural exceptions.

**SLIDE 5 — THE PATHWAYS**
- **Message:** Four distinct resilience pathways explain how recovery occurs.

- **Message:** Persistent constraints exist within specific country–commodity systems.
- **Focus:** Show Figure 05. 224 repeated Type C and 9 repeated Type D systems.

- **Message:** Capacity reductions shift the burden from existing networks to new origins.
- **Focus:** Show Figure 06 or 07. Under 0.25 HEEC, mean replacement remains 98.91%, but Type A falls to 79.98%.
- **Transition:** These counterfactuals offer clear guidance for policymakers.

**SLIDE 8 — THE INSIGHT**
- **Message:** "Concentration tells us where the shock starts. Trade-network structure tells us how recovery can occur."
- **Focus:** Summary of the core logical chain.
- **Transition:** How do we apply these insights?

**SLIDE 9 — POLICY IMPLICATIONS**
- **Message:** Focus on monitoring, diversification, historical fallbacks, and persistent constraints.
- **Focus:** Practical applications based solely on the model's evidence.
- **Transition:** Finally, we must acknowledge the boundaries of our study.

**SLIDE 10 — LIMITATIONS + CONCLUSION**
- **Message:** FOODSHIELD evaluates trade replacement feasibility, not guaranteed physical food security.
- **Focus:** Explicit statement of limitations (no price, no geopolitics, capacity proxy).
- **Final Conclusion:** Supplier concentration creates exposure, but exposure alone does not determine modeled recovery.
