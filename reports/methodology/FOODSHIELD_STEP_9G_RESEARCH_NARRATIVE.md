# FOODSHIELD — Research Narrative

## 1. Research Question
"When a country depends on one foreign supplier for an important food, can existing trade relationships replace most of the lost supply — or does resilience require a new origin?"

## 2. Problem Motivation
A country can appear exposed because a large share of its imports comes from one foreign supplier. But concentration alone does not tell us whether other current suppliers can expand, whether historical suppliers can return, whether new origins are required, or whether some losses remain unreplaced. Therefore, FOODSHIELD asks what happens AFTER the dominant supplier disappears. 

## 3. Why Supplier Concentration Is Not Enough
Different commodities exhibit substantial supplier concentration, but concentration is only the starting point. The important question is: "What happens after the shock?" Pre-shock concentration identifies exposure to a dominant supplier, but it does not reveal whether alternative trade relationships can absorb the lost supply.

## 4. Modeling the Supplier Shock
For each importer–commodity–year:
1. identify the largest foreign supplier
2. remove that supplier
3. calculate the lost share of baseline imports

This is an independent counterfactual shock. The modeled shock is substantial, with a mean Rank-1 shock loss of approximately 67.17%. Rice has the highest average modeled shock loss among the six commodities at approximately 77.9%.

## 5. Modeling Replacement
Replacement is modeled through a three-tier hierarchy based on the historical export-expansion capacity (HEEC) proxy:
- **Tier 1**: Current suppliers already supplying the importer–commodity in the shock year.
- **Tier 2**: Historical suppliers that supplied the importer–commodity in at least two previous years but were absent in the shock year.
- **Tier 3**: New origins with demonstrated global outward trade in the commodity but no qualifying previous bilateral relationship.

Replacement is not simply a question of whether enough trade exists globally; it is a question of how deeply the model must reach into existing, historical, and previously unused supplier relationships.

## 6. Main Findings
A large modeled supplier shock does NOT necessarily imply large unreplaced supply under the model. Across 10,953 capacity-valid Rank-1 supplier-shock scenarios (2011–2023), the mean modeled replacement rate is 99.83%. Most modeled Rank-1 shocks are fully replaceable using current-year existing suppliers, while most remaining scenarios can be resolved through historically observed suppliers or new origins.

## 7. Replacement Pathways
The model assigns scenarios into four pathways (resilience profiles):
- **TYPE A (87.04%)** - Existing-network resilient: The current supplier network can replace the shocked supply.
- **TYPE B (4.00%)** - Historically recoverable: Current suppliers alone cannot replace the loss, but historical supplier relationships can complete replacement.
- **TYPE C (8.55%)** - New-origin dependent: Current and historical relationships are insufficient, requiring new origins.
- **TYPE D (0.41%)** - Structurally constrained: Even the modeled new-origin pathway cannot completely replace the lost supply.

## 8. Persistent Country–Commodity Constraints
The majority of scenarios are resolved within the existing network, but persistent exceptions matter. Specifically, there are 224 country–commodity systems showing repeated Type C behavior and 9 country–commodity systems showing repeated Type D behavior. These represent country–commodity systems with repeated modeled difficulty in replacing the dominant supplier.

## 9. Robustness and Sensitivity
The headline modeled replacement result is robust to substantial reductions in the historical capacity proxy, but the pathway becomes more difficult as capacity is restricted. Under a 0.25 HEEC stress test scenario, the mean replacement rate is 98.91%, while Type A scenarios fall to 79.98%. Restricting modeled expansion capacity changes the pathway for some scenarios even though the aggregate replacement rate remains very high. For instance, 464 scenarios transition from A→C and 111 from C→D. 

## 10. Policy Implications
These results provide a basis for several policy implications:
- Monitor supplier concentration, not just total import dependence.
- Maintain multiple active supplier relationships where feasible.
- Preserve historically used supplier relationships that could provide fallback options.
- Identify country–commodity systems with repeated Type C/D behavior for deeper investigation.
- Treat new-origin diversification as particularly relevant where existing and historical supplier networks are insufficient.
- Avoid interpreting high aggregate replacement rates as proof of food security.
- Use capacity sensitivity to identify systems whose replacement pathway depends heavily on expansion assumptions.

## 11. Limitations
The model evaluates trade replacement feasibility under specified assumptions and does not predict future food security. The HEEC is a historical trade-expansion capacity proxy, not guaranteed spare physical capacity or actual future supplier willingness. The model does not directly represent prices, transport constraints, contracts, infrastructure, geopolitical disruptions, export restrictions, quality differences beyond commodity mapping, domestic production adaptation, commodity substitution, consumer-level food insecurity, or distributional inequality.

## 12. Conclusion
Supplier concentration creates exposure, but exposure alone does not determine modeled recovery. Across 10,953 capacity-valid Rank-1 supplier-shock scenarios, 99.83% of lost supply is replaced on average under the historical export-expansion capacity proxy. However, 87.04% of scenarios can be resolved through current suppliers alone, while 8.55% require new origins and 0.41% remain partially unreplaced. Restricting the capacity assumption reduces the modeled replacement rate only modestly but shifts more scenarios toward historically dependent, new-origin, or incomplete-replacement pathways.
