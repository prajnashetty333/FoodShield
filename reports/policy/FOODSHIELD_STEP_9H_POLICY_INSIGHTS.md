# FOODSHIELD — Policy Insights

## 1. Purpose
The purpose of this document is to translate the validated FOODSHIELD evidence into a rigorous, non-causal policy and decision framework. It answers the question: "Given the modeled replacement pathway for a supplier shock, what type of resilience action should decision-makers consider?"

## 2. Evidence Base
The framework is based entirely on 10,953 capacity-valid importer × commodity × year scenarios from 2011–2023 for six commodities (Wheat, Rice, Maize, Palm Oil, Sugar, Sunflower Oil). 99.83% of modeled supplier-shock losses are replaced on average under the historical export-expansion capacity proxy, following a Rank-1 supplier shock with a mean shock loss of approximately 67.17%.

## 3. Scope
The scope of this policy translation is limited to the trade-replacement feasibility of a Rank-1 supplier shock. It relies on the Historical Export Expansion Capacity (HEEC) proxy.

## 4. Four Resilience Profiles
- **TYPE A (Existing-network resilient)**: Current-year foreign suppliers other than the shocked supplier can fully replace the modeled lost supply under the HEEC capacity proxy.
- **TYPE B (Historically recoverable)**: Current-year suppliers cannot fully replace the modeled loss, but historically observed suppliers can complete replacement.
- **TYPE C (New-origin dependent)**: Current-year suppliers plus historically observed suppliers cannot fully replace the modeled loss, so Tier 3 new origins are required for full modeled replacement.
- **TYPE D (Structurally constrained)**: Even after considering current suppliers, historically observed suppliers, and qualifying new origins under the HEEC proxy, the modeled loss cannot be fully replaced.

## 5. Policy Interpretation
Import dependence tells us where exposure begins. FOODSHIELD asks whether that exposure is replaceable.

## 6. Profile-Specific Actions
- **Type A**:
  - maintain current supplier diversification
  - monitor supplier concentration
  - preserve functioning trade relationships
  - periodically reassess exposure
- **Type B**:
  - preserve historical supplier relationships
  - maintain market access
  - avoid unnecessary deterioration of previously active trade relationships
  - consider mechanisms that support supplier re-entry when appropriate
- **Type C**:
  - develop additional origins
  - diversify sourcing geography
  - qualify alternative exporters
  - reduce dependence on a narrow set of origins
  - consider trade-access diversification
- **Type D**:
  - investigate structural constraints
  - examine whether additional trade relationships are realistically available
  - assess infrastructure/logistics constraints externally
  - assess domestic contingency options externally
  - evaluate strategic contingency planning

## 8. Commodity-Level Interpretation
- **Wheat**: The modeled replacement pathways frequently rely on existing supplier networks. Policy implication: maintain supplier diversification and monitor concentration. Caution: Does not model domestic substitution.
- **Rice**: Exhibits relatively high modeled exposure to the loss of the largest supplier. Policy implication: Warrants further investigation into alternative supplier capacities. Caution: This does not prove Rice is more vulnerable beyond what existing datasets provide.
- **Maize**: Strong existing-network pathways. Policy implication: Maintain diverse supplier pools. Caution: Feed vs. food usage is not distinguished.
- **Palm Oil**: Concentrated supply globally. Policy implication: High relevance for new-origin development (Type C scenarios). Caution: HEEC proxy may overstate capacity if few dominant suppliers exist.
- **Sugar**: Historical supplier relationships contribute to modeled replacement in some scenarios. Policy implication: Historical supplier relationships (Type B) can provide strong fallbacks. Caution: Does not model long-term contracts.
- **Sunflower Oil**: Subject to regional concentration. Policy implication: Investigate structural bottlenecks when primary suppliers are lost. Caution: Ignores vegetable oil substitution.

## 9. Type D Diagnostic Framework
Type D indicates that the modeled replacement network remains insufficient even after considering current suppliers, historical suppliers, and qualifying new origins under the HEEC proxy.

Recommend investigation of:
- infrastructure
- logistics
- market access
- trade policy
- production constraints
- quality requirements
- transport costs
- geopolitical constraints
- contracts
- storage
- domestic contingency options

Explicitly state: These factors are not modeled by FOODSHIELD in the current version.

## 10. Sensitivity Implications
At 0.25 × HEEC:
- mean modeled replacement = 98.91%
- Type A = 79.98%
- Baseline A → C = 464 scenarios
- Baseline C → D = 111 scenarios

The headline replacement rate is robust, while the replacement pathway is more sensitive to capacity assumptions.

## 11. Monitoring Framework
Decision-maker checklist:
1. How concentrated are imports?
2. What share would be lost if the largest supplier disappeared?
3. Can current suppliers replace the modeled loss?
4. If not, can historical suppliers contribute?
5. Does full modeled replacement require a new origin?
6. Does any modeled loss remain unreplaced?
7. How sensitive is the pathway to capacity assumptions?
8. What external real-world constraints should be investigated?
9. What should be monitored over time?

## 12. External Information Required for Real-World Decisions
External data that policymakers would ideally examine outside FOODSHIELD:
- trade agreements
- tariffs
- logistics capacity
- port infrastructure
- transportation costs
- quality requirements
- procurement contracts
- political/geopolitical conditions

## 13. Limitations
HEEC is a historical export-expansion capacity proxy.
It is NOT:
- observed spare capacity
- guaranteed future capacity
- physical available stock
- contractual capacity
- infrastructure capacity

FOODSHIELD evaluates trade replacement feasibility, not food security itself. It does not predict future trade or identify causal drivers.

## 14. Non-Causal Interpretation
FOODSHIELD does not identify causal drivers. It translates modeled feasibility into decision directions. Type D identifies scenarios where the modeled trade-replacement pathways remain insufficient; additional investigation is required to determine why.

## 15. Final Policy Takeaway
FOODSHIELD does not simply identify where supplier concentration exists. It identifies how a modeled supplier shock can be replaced: through current suppliers, historically observed suppliers, or new origins — and flags the cases where even those modeled pathways remain insufficient.

A: Maintain and monitor existing networks.
B: Preserve or revive historically observed supplier relationships.
C: Develop additional origins.
D: Investigate structural constraints and contingency options.

These recommendations are decision-support implications of the modeled trade-replacement framework. They are not forecasts, causal conclusions, or guarantees of real-world food security.
